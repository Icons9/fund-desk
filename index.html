Landscape analysis — seven jurisdictions

Desk synthesis · researched Aug 2026 · full report in the data room

Assessed on ten parameters: AUM & growth · fundraising & dry powder · LP composition · regulation · tax · politics · people dynamics · returns/DPI · fund-services infrastructure · openness to first-time managers.

Executive summary

The question this analysis answers. We examined the private-markets landscape across seven jurisdictions — the USA, the EU, Luxembourg, Monaco, India, the GCC and China — through one consistent framework of ten parameters: market size and growth, fundraising climate, LP composition, regulation, tax, politics, people dynamics, returns and DPI, fund-services infrastructure, and openness to first-time managers. The purpose was not description but decision: to stress-test every assumption our venture rests on, and to say where the evidence should change our behaviour.

The single most important finding is that the global industry has split into two viable positions — very large and very specialist — with the middle collapsing. In 2025 the ten largest funds absorbed roughly 46% of all capital raised, while first-time managers in North America collected just $7.2bn, down about a third, and Europe's ~95 debut managers shared roughly $7.5bn. Yet within that brutal market, specialist first-timers kept closing, often oversubscribed. The implication for us is not motivational but structural: a small, sharply specialised, anchor-led fund is the only configuration of our idea that the market currently funds. Any drift toward mid-size generalism is a drift toward the one position the data shows dying on every continent.

The second finding is about cash. Across the West, distributions have run at roughly 8–10% of NAV for three years — half the historical norm — leaving LPs locked in and skeptical; the gap has been patched by a record continuation-vehicle and secondaries market rather than by real exits. India shows the same disease differently: a spectacular IPO year in 2025, but older fund vintages that returned only 0.5–0.8x of capital, teaching Indian LPs to distrust paper IRR. The consequence is that the next fundraising cycle everywhere belongs to strategies that pay cash early. This is a direct tailwind for our credit-anchored design, and it argues for re-weighting our pitch language from terminal IRR toward yield, income and early DPI.

The third finding is that our chosen geography is where the growth is. The traditional LP base — Western pensions and endowments — is at or above allocation targets and shrinking as a marginal buyer. The growing pools are insurance balance sheets, sovereign wealth, family offices and, progressively, retail wealth platforms. Two of those four are precisely our base: Indian family offices have grown from roughly 45 to 300 since 2018 and now anchor most new domestic vintages, while the Gulf hosts nearly 1,300 family-related entities in DIFC alone and the UAE was again the world's leading destination for migrating millionaires — with Indians the largest cohort. Our diaspora-first thesis is, on the evidence, aimed at the fastest-compounding LP demography in the world.

Regionally, the USA functions as our leading indicator rather than our market: what begins there — retail 401(k) access, insurance-fed credit platforms, continuation-vehicle liquidity — reaches Europe and India two to three years later. Europe is our asset market, and its numbers strengthened our thesis materially: a record €147bn raised in 2025 and, most strikingly, 46% of global private-credit fundraising captured by Europe as Basel IV pushed banks out of mid-market lending. AIFMD II takes effect in April 2026, before our launch, which means we build under settled rules rather than shifting ones. Luxembourg validated itself with alternatives growing ~20% in a year to €3.1tn; its only warnings are operational — compliance-talent scarcity and rising service costs, which we should pre-empt by locking provider quotes early. Monaco's story is timing: with its FATF action plan substantially complete, grey-list exit is plausible within two to three quarters, which converts our already-chosen deferral of the Monaco company from caution into strategy. India delivered the strongest growth signals of the seven — AIF commitments up 25% to ~₹17tn, a record $12.4bn of private credit deployed, GIFT City functioning at scale with more than $5bn of foreign inflows — alongside the clearest warnings: exit dependence on a cooling IPO window, currency drag, and a professionalising spin-out wave that is simultaneously our competitor set and our Fund-Manager recruiting pool. The GCC emerged as arguably our most important LP geography: sovereign capital is pivoting domestic and demanding localisation, but family capital is abundant, mobile and concentrated in Dubai — and it negotiates for exactly the things our loyalty architecture already offers, above all co-investment. China, finally, is closed to managers of our size — USD fundraising has collapsed to a decade low — and matters to us only through the rotation of global Asia allocations toward India, which quietly works in our favour.

Re-testing our assumptions, every structural choice survived: the Luxembourg RAIF wrapper, the credit anchor, the dual maritime and healthcare/deeptech specialisation, the deferred Monaco entity, the GIFT City rail, and the diaspora LP focus. Three assumptions, however, should be held loosely. The €5–25m first close is achievable but only anchor-led — the cornerstone LPs are not an accelerant, they are the raise itself. The India feeder should be built against documented demand, not in advance of it, because the 2025 IFSCA regime added real compliance cost. And our return language should lead with income rather than IRR, because that is what every region's LPs now buy. We should also respect one risk we cannot diversify: the first genuine private-credit default cycle, which European regulators now openly anticipate — our conservative underwriting standards are not a marketing point but the condition of survival.

What we should do reduces to five moves. Stay the course on structure and thesis, but sharpen execution around anchors and DPI-first messaging. Add a deliberate Gulf rhythm now — regular Dubai presence, DIFC relationships, co-invest-rich terms. Time-box the GIFT City feeder to evidence of demand. Design fund terms so a future wealth-channel or ELTIF-compatible sleeve is possible without committing to one. And keep a watching brief on London's 2028 regime as a possible Fund II distribution hedge. The overall conclusion is unusual for a landscape review: the environment is harsher than headlines suggest for new managers in general, yet almost every specific bet this venture has made sits on the right side of the industry's divergences. The task is not to change the plan; it is to execute it with the discipline the data demands.
